Executive Summary

CradleWave is a land-based sailing training device designed to solve a problem that no product on the market currently addresses: the inability to effectively practice critical sailing maneuvers, specifically the roll tack, outside of on-water conditions. By replicating the lateral weight-transfer mechanics of a roll tack on dry land, CradleWave gives sailing teams the ability to build muscle memory and refine technique anytime, regardless of weather, water access, or scheduling constraints.

CradleWave enters a market with no main competitors, as there’s currently no reliable on-land product to help sailors master the roll-tack maneuver. With over 1000 sailing organizations and programs in the United States, it’s rather peculiar that there is no real tool for land-based maneuver training.

In terms of CradleWave’s product design, the key characteristic is that its minimalist design opens further potential and opportunities for various versions or customization, and this is precisely what gives the product opportunity for continuous innovation and profit. For example, future iterations could include adjustable heel-stop positions beyond the current three-angle configuration, modular attachment points for telemetry sensors, or team-branded colorways, all achievable within the existing PVC cradle framework without redesigning completely from scratch. This modularity also creates a tiered product strategy: a base model priced for high school teams, and an instrumented or competition-spec version targeting collegiate programs with larger equipment budgets, both built on the same core platform.

The Need

The need we identified through our research was that there is no effective way for sailors to practice the roll tack technique on land. Roll tacking is a fundamental sailing technique in which sailors shift their weight within the boat to maintain speed and efficiency whilst changing direction. This technique is important for competitive sailors to learn. However, it is difficult to teach and learn because so far it has only been practiced effectively on water under appropriate weather conditions. As a result, sailors then have very limited opportunities to hone the skill, which in turn slows the learning process and reduces the effectiveness of training sessions.

The observations using the AEIOU framework and interviews conducted with the Northwestern Sailing Team at the sailing center highlighted that their current training methods are very reliant on verbal communication and demonstrations on land. These demonstrations mimic the sailing movements on land by having members of the sailing team pick up and move the boat themselves, though they fail to replicate the dynamic rolling motion of the boat on water, creating unrealistic motion and sailing conditions.

 During an interview, one sailor described roll tacking as a "make-or-break" skill for competitive performance and emphasized that there is currently no meaningful way to practice it on land. Another sailor stated that beginners often require several months to consistently perform a successful roll tack and expressed a strong interest in a simulator that would allow additional practice opportunities on land. These interviews confirmed both the importance of the skill and the demand for a training solution.

The journey map (Fig.1) developed from observations and interviews with the Northwestern Sailing Team provided a more detailed understanding of how the sailors currently attempt to practice roll tacking on land. The process begins when team members arrive at the beach, retrieve a boat, and position it on the sand. Two sailors then stand on opposite sides of the boat and manually lift and tip it to simulate the rolling motion experienced during a roll tack. To practice the maneuver realistically, an additional sailor is often needed to sit inside the boat while the others create the rolling motion. Once the boat is positioned, sailors can briefly rehearse the body movements involved in roll tacking.

 Figure 1: Current user journey map to practice roll tacking

Though several significant pain points emerged throughout the process. First, practicing requires multiple people, making it difficult for individual sailors to train independently. Second, manually lifting and tipping the boat is physically demanding and can become tiring after only a short period of time. Finally, because the process relies on other team members and requires repeatedly moving a heavy boat, it is not highly repeatable and limits the amount of practice that can realistically occur during a training session.

Thus, this journey map identified the opportunity during the practice stage where sailors could use a dedicated roll tack simulator that would eliminate the need for multiple helpers, provide a way to repeatedly train and practice safely, while focusing on developing the skills required to perform a roll tack.

The user persona defined for our opportunity space was centered around the members of Northwestern’s Sailing Team. Since the roll-tacking maneuver is specifically utilized by experienced sailors in a competitive environment, we defined that our final product would focus on sailors intending to practice roll tacking for competitive purposes. Related to the early problem of limited on-water practice time, our persona reflected a competitive sailor who is technically capable but constrained by environment and scheduling: someone who knows what the technique requires but lacks the repetitions needed to build muscle memory.

The Design

Design Requirements & Constraints

        The design requirements and constraints for our design process were derived from our user research with the Northwestern Sailing Team, physical constraints imposed by the 420 sailboat itself, and practical limitations of our budget and timeline. These were formalized into a specification chart (Appendix A) with needs, metrics, units, and marginal and ideal values.

Table 1. Spec Chart, Requirements and Constraints

Requirement/Constraint

Explanation

Rolling Motion

The most critical user requirement was that the simulator must replicate the rolling motion of a sailboat during a roll tack. From interviews with the sailing team, the maximum heel angle during a roll tack was estimated at no more than 55°, with research on roll tack technique identifying 45° as the target functional heel range. Thus, our final design was required to reach a full 45° heel on each side.

Structural Load Capacity

The cradle must support the weight of the 420 sailboat hull (approx. 230 lbs as specified by the manufacturer), as well as the weight of two adult sailors.

Structural Safety

A sufficient factor of safety (FOS) was required across all load-bearing elements to ensure the structure remained safe under all expected training conditions.

Motion Quality

In addition to the heel angle, the rolling motion also needed to feel realistic enough for meaningful skill transfer. A good roll tack requires a well-timed weight shift, a controlled exit, and a return to flat without overcorrection. The simulator must not produce an unrealistically easy or abrupt transition.

Interior Accuracy

For training to be transferable to sailing on water, sailors needed to access the actual features of the boat (ropes, straps, tiller, etc.) during practice. Any solution that replaced or changed these elements would reduce training value.

Boat Security and Usability

The solution must hold the boat securely during use. Setup time and ease of securing the boat would also be tracked.

User Safety

Exposed pinch points between the boat and any structural elements needed to be minimized, as sailors must be able to perform the roll motion freely without the risk of contact with the frame.

Durability and Environmental Resistance

Given that the simulator would be used outdoors at the sailing center, all materials needed to withstand repeated exposure to water, sand, and sun, without significant degradation.

Trainability

The ultimate measure of success was whether the simulator would meaningfully reduce the time needed to develop roll tack competency compared to existing land-based methods.

Concept Development 

        Three main design concepts were developed and evaluated, assessed using a weighted concept grading matrix (Appendix B). Criteria were prioritized to reflect the needs identified by the Northwestern Sailing Team: rolling motion capability, interior accuracy for footwork practice, and force/resistance simulation. Feasibility constraints (budget, technical skillset, timeline) were also included as criteria.  

Concept 1: Rocking Cradle

        The rocking cradle concept places the real 420 sailboat into a curved or angled external frame, allowing it to rock from side to side under sailor weight shifts. This concept addressed all three feasibility criteria (budget, skillset, timeline) and directly satisfied the two highest-priority user needs of the rolling motion and interior accuracy, since it uses the actual boat. Its key shortcoming was that it cannot accurately simulate hydrodynamic forces or resistance, and that it rolls on only one axis, limiting the range of maneuvers that can be practiced. Open design questions included what profile geometry would produce the correct heel angle range, how to secure the boat within the cradle, and whether the frame bottom could be shaped to allow for limited pitch or yaw motion as well.

Concept 2: Full Boat Pivot Mount

        The pivot mount concept suspends the 420 from a central pivot point on a fixed frame, allowing the boat to tip side to side about that axis. Like the cradle, it satisfied the rolling motion and accurate interior requirements. It also fits within the team's technical skillset. However, it raised concerns about budget and timeline feasibility, as fabricating a reliable pivot assembly capable of handling the full load of the boat and two sailors introduced significant mechanical complexity. Additional design questions included how to prevent the overall frame from tipping, how to constrain the heel range to a safe limit, and how to keep the pivot connection stable under dynamic loading. Like the cradle, it could not simulate water resistance forces.

Concept 3: Resistance Simulation

        This concept focused on replicating the hydrodynamic resistance feel of a roll tack using a magnetic braking mechanism, rather than physically rocking the actual boat. It was the only concept that directly addressed the resistance simulation need. However, it carried the most shortcomings: it did not have an accurate interior, raised concerns about budget, technical feasibility, and timeline, and the scalability of the magnetic force from a small prototype to a full-scale system was unresolved. Without a realistic interior, the concept could not support the footwork and line-handling practice that the sailing team identified as essential.

These concepts were evaluated individually by each team member using the weighted grading matrix, then aggregated into a composite score. The Rocking Cradle emerged as the clear top performer across all five individual scorecards, driven by its strong performance on the two highest-weighted user priorities and its feasibility across all three constraint criteria. This directed the team toward the cradle approach as the basis for the final design, with subsequent prototyping and testing focused on resolving the open design questions the concept raised.

Final Design and Design Rationale

For our final design, we decided to go with a cradle design using a PVC exoskeleton approach. PVC was chosen for its excellent strength, durability, and hollow nature. The exoskeleton-style design and PVC pipe structure provide great flexibility, as they allow us to adjust our rolling physics post-construction by adding weighted sandbags externally or water internally. Other materials that were considered but not chosen were wooden 2x8 planks and plywood, which would have been easier to construct but were expected to last for a much shorter time on the beach than PVC pipes due to their sensitivity to humidity and water. A general overview of the CradleWave Design can be seen in Fig. 2.

Figure 2: Final Design with 420 Sailboat in Solidworks

The PVC exoskeleton is divided into five ribbed sections, with four perpendicular rib connector pipes connecting each rib to the next. Each rib is divided into three sections, with a boat support section jutting up and out of the rib to support the 420, an inner profile for structural stability, and an outer profile for the cradle to roll on. These sections can be seen in Figure 3. Its outer and inner profiles are divided into five sections, one perpendicular to the ground to reflect a flat boat position, two are at 22.5° of the flat section to reflect a slight heel, and two are at 45° of the flat section to reflect a more extreme heel. The position and length of each section of the outer profile relative to the boat were all chosen using COM calculations, using the expected position of sailors in each type of heel as seen in (Appendix C). Each section on the outer profile is connected to a parallel section on the inner profile via two vertical connectors to create a strong load-bearing square shape. Additionally, the vertical connectors were positioned near the angled transition fittings, where the outer rolling profile changes direction, in order to reinforce the bend locations and to decrease the moment arm on the fittings.

Figure 3: PVC Exoskeleton in Solidworks

Figure 4: PVC front view in Solidworks

For all five sections on the outer/inner profiles, one of the vertical connectors is directly in line with a boat support, which is constructed out of a PVC pipe connected to a table end cap that is screwed into a wooden block with a block of foam glued to it, as seen in Figure 5. The wooden block gives support and structure to the foam, which adapts to the shape of the boat and also gives the foam block a way to be secured to the PVC table end cap. The lengths of each boat support were determined via CAD using an imported 420 hull file. Since the boat supports are in line with vertical connectors, for each roll position, the boat supports are aligned with the section of the rib that carries the load in that orientation. In the flat position, the hull load transfers nearly right down through the flat boat supports into a vertical connector to the flat outer profile section and into the ground. At the 22.5° and 45° degree heels, the same load path logic applies: the corresponding angled supports direct the load into the matching angled profile section. This creates a continuous, nearly axial load path through the PVC pipes, minimizing bending in the pipes and reducing stress concentrations at the fittings.

Works-Like Prototype & Testing

Final Prototype Description and Manufacturing

The final prototype translated the PVC exoskeleton cradle design and CAD model into a full-scale, physical structure capable of supporting a 420 sailboat on land. While the previous section explains the design rationale behind the cradle geometry, this section focuses on the completed prototype, how it was manufactured, how the boat interfaces with it, and how it is used during roll tack and heel simulation. The final prototype of the CradleWave that we delivered to the Northwestern Sailing Center and Northwestern Sailing Team can be seen here in Fig. 6.

Figure 6: Overview of Final CradleWave Prototype

To use the CradleWave, one must place the front of a 420 sailboat directly behind the CradleWave and then lift it and carry it into place onto the boat supports. Then, using 2 ratchet straps, one must secure the boat solidly into place by wrapping one around the bow and the front rib connectors and one around the stern and back rib connectors. Once the 420 is secured in place and solidly contacting all boat supports, the CradleWave is safe to use.

Figure 7: CradleWave Setup & Loading

The primary use of the CradleWave is to simulate roll tacks on land, which it can do unassisted thanks to the design of its outer rolling profile. The secondary use is as a general heel simulator, which it can also do with the assistance of 1 or 2 extra people manually adjusting and guiding the heel of the cradlewave. This secondary use allows it to be used to simulate many other sailing maneuvers, such as standard tacks, tubes, and pumping, to name a few.

                        

To construct our final prototype as fast and efficiently as possible, we decided to follow our CAD as closely as possible. We first began by measuring the pipe lengths and number/type of fittings included on one rib, then cut the pipes and prepped the fittings for 1 rib, and finished by dry fitting our first rib. After careful examination, we made some slight adjustments to our pipe lengths and updated our measurements for the remaining pipe. Once the final pipe lengths were confirmed, we proceeded to cut out the rest of the pipes and locate and prepare the fittings for our remaining ribs. To assemble our ribs, we started by first creating smaller independent sections as seen in Fig. 8, which were then slowly added together from the center out. Once each rib was constructed, we then cut out our rib connectors and connected all 5 ribs to each other. Then, using our CAD assembly again, we measured, cut, and prepped the proper lengths for the boat support pipes, and cut out our wooden and foam blocks. The foam blocks were then glued to the wooden ones, the table end caps were then screwed to the wooden blocks, and the pipes were added to the end caps and structure in the correct locations.

 

Figure 8: Rib Assembly Progress

We then carried the entire prototype from Ford to the sailing center on dollies and secured the CadleWave to them with ratchet straps as seen in Figure 9. Once the CradleWave was delivered to the sailing center, it was placed on the beach, and a 420 was placed in it to check fitment. Some boat supports did not fully contact the 420’s hull, so the boat was removed, and extra foam was added to said supports. The boat was placed on the CradleWave again to ensure the modifications were adequate, and we confirmed this.

Figure 9: CradleWave Prototype Delivery

Figure 10: CradleWave Support Alterations - Original Length (Green), Updated Length (Yellow)

To ensure safe user testing, we then secured the 420 to the cradlewave using ratchet straps and began to slowly rock the empty boat back and forth, increasing its intensity and speed the longer we went. Once we had confirmed that the empty boat could easily swing from each 45-degree heel to the other aggressively without any creaking, shifting, or slipping, we then felt comfortable moving onto user and functional testing.

User & Functional Testing

        The final prototype was brought to the beach at the Northwestern Sailing Center to enable user testing. We were able to test with two members of the sailing team, walking them through the entire process of using the CradleWave, from setup to takedown.

It took about six people to set up the CradleWave, by lifting the 420 sailboat through the back of the CradleWave, occasionally resting it on the central supports while sailors adjusted positions around it to continue moving the boat into position on the CradleWave. The roughly 300-pound boat needs to be carefully lifted into the frame of the CradleWave to avoid dragging it over the supports, which could rip off the foam support pads, or more concerningly, break a support pole. This part of the setup process is by far the most tedious, laborious, and error-prone part of using the CradleWave. After this difficult step, it is relatively simple to ratchet the boat down to secure it in the CradleWave.

With the sailboat ratcheted into the CradleWave frame, sailors can pull one side of the frame down, tipping the boat to allow them to climb in. At this point, the sailors are able to practice any drills they want as if they are on the water, including rolltacking. For roll tacking specifically, the sailors noted that the CradleWave would be great for training new sailors and that it is very useful for practicing footwork. The main critiques of the CradleWave for roll tacking is that it may be too difficult to reach the 45-degree heel and that the CradleWave rolls too far past the flat angle when coming out of the roll tack. Both issues may potentially be alleviated by attaching weights (like sandbags) to the connecting pipes on one side (the heel side) of the CradleWave. This will need to be tested in the future.

Figure 11: CradleWave User Testing with NU Sailing Team

Finally, the sailors were able to remove the sailboat from the CradleWave using about six people and a similar method as was used to get the boat into the frame. This presented similar pain points. However, it is also important to note that if the boat does not need to be used elsewhere, it can be safely left in the CradleWave’s frame for storage.

Summary of Early Prototypes

        Before building the full-scale CradleWave, we created small-scale models at approximately one-twentieth and one-fifth scale using 3D printing and laser cutting, respectively. The 3D printed models (Fig. 12) were used to experiment with different profiles for the bottom of the CradleWave. These models showed that smooth curves allowed for too easy transitions between the heeled and flat positions. This pushed us to use flat planes in our laser-cut models. The first laser-cut model (Fig. 13) had one single 45-degree angle between the flat and heeled positions, but this transition proved far too abrupt in user testing. This led to us using two 22.5-degree angles between the flat and heeled positions for our next laser-cut model. This model received good feedback from members of the sailing team, and a highly similar profile was used for our final prototype.

Business Model Canvas: Sections 

Key Partners  

The key partners identified for CradleWave are primarily centered around competitive sailing programs and production hardware. Specific to our design, hardware suppliers are central to the building and production process because the entire device is built from PVC materials that are widely available and consistently stocked, meaning there’s a very low chance of supply shortages that would disrupt production or replication. Given our focus on competitive sailing programs, we want to pursue a partnership with the Intercollegiate Sailing Association (ICSA), the governing body for collegiate sailing in the United States. A formal relationship with ICSA could potentially reduce the efforts required to reach the target market for our product in the first place and significantly accelerate the adoption of this new product within the sailing scene.

Key Activities 

The key activities for our current phase are centered around iterative user testing and development with the Northwestern Sailing Team. While we’ve tested the functionality of our product and have determined the future possibilities with design variations as a market opportunity, we’re currently keeping an eye out for any potential issues with hardware and part replacement. We plan to consistently maintain contact with the Northwestern Sailing Team as they incorporate CradleWave into their regular practices, maintaining feedback loops to keep tabs on what is working and what is not.

Value Proposition

The value proposition of CradleWave is the ability to practice roll tacking on land, which currently cannot be done efficiently/effectively, along with the fact that there are no recent designs or products meant to address this concern. The current approach to land-based roll tack practice requires multiple people and significant physical effort, and still fails to properly replicate the dynamic motions of the boat on water. CradleWave provides a relief to these barriers by introducing a way to practice roll tacking independently, repeatedly, and at any time, regardless of weather or water conditions. In addition, as mentioned above, the current PVC design paves the way for customization that alters the way the rolling motion feels or makes it more difficult for the sailor to complete a roll tack, as well as team-branded customization.

Customer Relationships and Channels

Purchasing decisions for training equipment within collegiate and high school sailing programs are typically made by the head coach, and as a result, the primary customer relationship for CradleWave is coach-mediated or captain-mediated. However, we would like to expand beyond coaches and reach sailors who use the simulator to advocate for it within their programs, so the relationship starts athlete-up rather than coach-down. Since the product was built through multiple iterations of user testing with real competitive sailors, this athlete-up approach also reinforces a core part of CradleWave's identity: that it is a training tool designed around the usability and comfort of sailors themselves, not a product designed in a vacuum and handed down to them.

In line with this athlete-up approach, our primary channel is a direct outreach strategy to student athletes through demonstrations at regional sailing events where sailors themselves are present. The goal is for these student athletes to become the first point of contact with the product, building enthusiasm and familiarity with the purpose and functionality of the product before any official discussion of purchase. This is particularly specified for a collegiate sailing context, where team culture and peer influence play a significant role in how training methods and equipment are acquired and utilized.

Revenue Streams

CradleWave’s primary revenue stream is direct unit sales to sailing programs and teams. At the moment, pricing has not yet been finalized and will be set following a second build that establishes a more accurate per-unit cost baseline. Two revenue streams that are currently under consideration are the direct sale of a completed unit and a building kit, where customers receive pre-cut materials and assembly instructions.

Aside from direct product sales, the brand equity analysis identified three partnership directions that each represent a distinct secondary revenue stream. A collaboration with Zim Sailing, whose mission statement around accessibility and youth development directly aligns with CradleWave’s value proposition, would position the product as a Zim-claimed coaching tool to the stakeholders of Zim boats. Another partnership consideration is Red Bull, as it would reposition the product toward a performance and competitive audience, with design modifications such as steeper rolling, additional degrees of freedom, and other design considerations to create a more extreme training experience consistent with Red Bull’s sponsorship portfolio. Finally, some sort of furniture company collaboration would allow for an emphasis on customization, premium materials, and aesthetic finish to produce a version of CradleWave suited to home gyms and private training spaces, targeting individual competitive sailors rather than institutional organizations/programs.

Cost Structure

The cost structure for CradleWave currently consists of materials, including PVC pipe and fittings, hardware, foam padding, ratchet straps, and other parts. The first prototype built resulted in just over $1000, and currently, we plan to make the product at a fixed cost with the finalized pricing being entirely tied to the cost of materials. Going forward, we plan to identify where costs can be reduced through optimized material selection and bulk purchasing, which would inform the final pricing structure.

Market Size and Pricing Strategy

While pricing has not been set and will be determined when cost refinement is complete, taking into consideration the current market landscape and the brand equity direction, we determined that CradleWave’s pricing strategy will need to be accommodating and flexible enough to serve multiple audience segments, ranging from high school sailing programs with a more limited budget to collegiate sailing teams with larger equipment budgets.

Ethical and Professional Responsibilities

Ethical Considerations in Engineering Decisions

Developing a physical training device that holds a ~300 lb sailboat with sailors aboard requires careful consideration of safety at every stage of design. The primary ethical obligation of the CradleWave team was to ensure that no user could be harmed by normal or reasonably foreseeable use of the device. This shaped decisions around structural load capacity, frame geometry, foam padding placement, and ratchet strap securing mechanisms throughout the design process.

A secondary ethical consideration was training fidelity. If the CradleWave teaches sailors incorrect technique, whether through an unrealistic motion profile, inaccurate resistance feel, or improper body positioning cues, it could actively harm their on-water performance and potentially create unsafe habits in a competitive sailing environment. This risk elevated the importance of iterative user testing with the Northwestern Sailing Team, whose feedback directly shaped the profile geometry and heel-stop angle configuration of the final design.

Both categories of risk (physical safety and training integrity) were formally documented using a Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA), discussed further below.

ESG Impacts

Environmental: The CradleWave is constructed primarily from PVC pipe, which carries environmental considerations both in manufacture and end-of-life disposal. PVC production involves chlorine chemistry and generates toxic byproducts, and the material is not widely recyclable in standard municipal streams. These are acknowledged limitations of the material choice, selected primarily for its cost, structural performance, weather resistance, and availability. Future iterations could explore alternative structural materials with lower environmental impact, such as aluminum extrusion or sustainably sourced wood framing. On the positive side, the CradleWave extends the useful training life of existing 420 sailboats already owned by sailing programs, reducing the need for additional equipment purchases and associated material consumption.

Social: By enabling land-based roll tack practice, the CradleWave has the potential to reduce barriers to skill development for sailors who lack consistent access to on-water training time due to weather, scheduling, or geography. This is particularly relevant for high school and collegiate programs with limited coaching staff or shared water access. A more accessible training tool supports broader participation in competitive sailing and reduces the advantage gap between well-resourced and under-resourced programs.

Governance: The CradleWave was designed with and for the Northwestern Sailing Team, with their input embedded throughout the design process via interviews, observations, and direct user testing. This participatory approach reflects a commitment to designing with users rather than for them, and to making informed decisions about tradeoffs, such as the choice to prioritize rolling motion and interior accuracy over resistance simulation, transparently and with user buy-in.

Professional Responsibilities and Informed Judgement

As engineering designers, the team held a responsibility to make decisions grounded in evidence rather than assumptions. Key decisions, including the heel-stop angle configuration, structural member sizing, and foam pad attachment method, were each supported by either user research, physical calculations, or prototype testing. Where uncertainty remained, as in the long-term durability of foam padding under repeated loading and the accuracy of the rolling motion feel, those limitations were documented and communicated openly rather than minimized.

The team also held a responsibility to the sailing team as stakeholders who trusted us with access to their equipment, their time, and their physical safety during user testing. This meant being transparent about the prototype’s limitations during testing sessions, not overrepresenting what the device could do, and incorporating critical feedback into the documented next steps for the design rather than dismissing it.

Mitigation Strategies and Responsible Innovation

The FMEA (Appendix D) conducted for CradleWave identified failure modes across two categories: device failures and user failures. All identified failure modes fell within the low risk range (RPN ≤ 39) under the team's rating criteria, indicating that the current design's existing controls are adequate, though continued monitoring and future refinement are warranted.

The highest-severity device failure modes were boat slippage from the cradle (S=5, caused by insufficient ratchet strap tension) and frame fracture under excessive load (S=5). Both are currently controlled through fit testing and load testing, respectively, and both were addressed in the design through the ratchet strap securing system and the structural reinforcement of the PVC frame at bend locations. For the user failure category, the most critical mode was boat slippage leading to injury risk (S=5, RPN=30), again controlled by the ratchet strap system and fit testing.

The most frequently occurring user failure mode was unrealistic resistance or motion feel leading to incorrect technique development (O=4), reflecting the inherent limitation of a gravity-driven cradle system. This was mitigated through iterative user testing and profile refinement across multiple scaled mockups before committing to the full-scale build. Remaining open risks, including long-term foam pad wear, the potential need for counterweights to achieve consistent 45° heel, and the setup complexity flagged by users, represent clear directions for responsible continued development of the CradleWave platform.

2. Corrected CAD translation: edge-bounded rocker stability

Correction: the rocker planes must match the lower curve of the boat/rocker, not invert over it. The center face is flat and lowest. Moving outward, the contact profile rises through the 22.5 degree face and then the 45 degree face. In rough ASCII, the corrected lower profile is like \ _ //, not the opposite orientation.

Important design intent: this CAD section is a generic construction example only. It does not require your final PVC spacing, support height, or final bend locations to match any earlier example. The point is to show how one would check edge-bounded stability once the actual CAD profile exists.

2.1 Use the two edges of each face as the stability plane

For a given face, do not target a midpoint. Instead, use the two physical edges of that face as the boundaries of the support zone. When that face is the one touching the ground/platform, draw vertical construction lines upward from both face edges.

Edge-zone rule: Inside the two edge lines = held on that face; left of the left edge line = rolls onward to the next more-heeled face; right of the right edge line = rolls back to the previous less-heeled face.

2.2 Apply this only to the 22.5 degree and 45 degree faces

The example check below uses only the 22.5 degree and 45 degree faces. In CAD, create a display state, construction sketch, or copied sketch where the face being checked is horizontal. Then draw the vertical edge lines upward from the two endpoints of that face and inspect where each stage COM projection falls.

Generic CAD workflow

  • Draw the corrected outer rocker contact profile: center flat, then 22.5 degree faces rising outward, then 45 degree faces rising outward. The right side visually rises like /; the left side is mirrored like \.
  • Choose the actual PVC/bend spacing and support height based on construction constraints. Do not force the old example coordinates if they do not match the built frame.
  • For the 22.5 degree face, rotate or orient the sketch so that the 22.5 degree face is flat/horizontal. Draw vertical lines upward from the two endpoints of that face.
  • Check whether the 22.5 degree stage COM falls inside that zone. Check whether the next-stage COM is left of the zone if you want it to roll onward, and whether the previous-stage COM is right of the zone if you want it to roll back.
  • Repeat the same edge-boundary check for the 45 degree face. The 45 degree face should be a controlled heel/limit face, not an inverted roof-like segment.
  • After the outer profile is set, place the inner PVC profile and braces to support the outer edge nodes. For PVC, it is acceptable to prioritize clean T/cross connectors and direct load paths over a perfect geometric offset.

2.3 PVC inner profile note

For the inner PVC profile, use the corrected outer contact profile as the reference. The inner structure should sit inside/above the lower contact profile and should support the important outer edge nodes. Vertical or angled web members can be placed from the outer face edges down/up to the inner rail depending on the fittings you can actually build. The key is that the outer contact planes keep the correct orientation: flat center, then 22.5, then 45 as they rise outward.

Executive Summary

Overview

The CradleWave allows both experienced and novice sailors to practice rolltacking, and various other sailing maneuvers, on land for when weather conditions are too rough for actual sailing. By allowing sailors to train off the water, the CradleWave reduces lost training time and accelerates skill development.

The Problem

Sailors in regions with frequently inclement weather lose sailing time and the ability to practice essential maneuvers for competitive sailing. This affects both experienced sailors seeking to further perfect their movements, and beginners who are just trying to get the basics. Enabling the practice of roll tacking on land would allow for sailors to develop skills much more reliably and consistently.

Our Solution

The CradleWave enables the practice of roll tacking (and many other sailing maneuvers) on land by creating a frame in which the boat can safely roll from side to side. By using the actual boat in its frame, the CradleWave allows for the simulator to have a perfectly realistic interior. This makes footwork and line-handling practice in the CradleWave meaningful, as it matches exactly what sailors use on the water.

Market Analysis

While this design was driven by needs found at the Northwestern University Sailing Center, there are approximately 800 high school and collegiate sailing teams in the US who would likely benefit from affordable, physical, simulation. Since the market for physical sailing simulators is relatively uncompetitive, with most existing simulators being out of production, the CradleWave has the opportunity to carve out a significant portion of the market for physical sailing simulators.
